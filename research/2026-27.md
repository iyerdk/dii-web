**Key facts (5 bullets):**
- FCC voted unanimously 3-0 on June 25, 2026 to adopt the Second Report and Order and Further NPRM on submarine cable landing licences
- SLTE (Submarine Line Terminal Equipment) licensing: all hardware at US cable-landing points converting optical to terrestrial signals now requires FCC licence — Huawei, ZTE, and HMN Technologies (Huawei Marine Networks) barred from ownership
- Team Telecom fast-track: cables meeting high security certification standards are presumptively exempt from full Team Telecom national security review
- Primary SLTE beneficiaries: Nokia Submarine Networks, Subcom, Alcatel Submarine Networks — the only eligible hardware suppliers at US-landing cable points
- Further NPRM opens consultation on expanding FCC authority further into cable infrastructure regulation

**Research narrative (150–200 words):**
The FCC's unanimous June 25 adoption of the Second R&O completes the US regulatory layer of the three-tier governance framework that now governs global submarine cable infrastructure. The order operates on two levels simultaneously. First, it extends FCC authority to the physical equipment layer — SLTE hardware at US cable-landing points — locking out Chinese-connected suppliers and cementing Nokia Submarine Networks, Subcom, and Alcatel Submarine Networks as the only eligible hardware vendors at US termination points. Second, it creates a fast-track for cables that self-certify to high security standards, allowing US hyperscalers funding private cable systems (Google Firmina, Meta 2Africa) to avoid lengthy and unpredictable Team Telecom reviews.

The combined effect is to make US-connected cables simultaneously harder for adversaries to access and faster for trusted operators to deploy. With the EU Cable Security Toolbox (civilian infrastructure), GUIDE (17-nation military/intelligence), and now the FCC SLTE+fast-track order (equipment + licensing layers), the global submarine cable governance architecture is operationally complete for the first time.

Watch: whether the Further NPRM expands FCC authority to satellite-terrestrial cable junctions, and whether Ofcom or the European Commission legislate equivalent SLTE-type equipment control at European cable-landing points.

### Energy & Power: Germany's PUE ≤1.2 and ERF ≥10% mandates trigger July 1, 2026 — liquid cooling paradox emerges

Thread: eu-dc-efficiency-standards
Sources:
- https://www.whitecase.com/insight-alert/data-center-requirements-under-new-german-energy-efficiency-act
- https://www.wfw.com/articles/data-centres-an-international-legal-and-regulatory-perspective-spotlight-on-germany/
- https://digitalisationworld.com/blog/58424/is-the-german-energy-efficiency-act-aligned-to-data-centre-growth/

**Key facts (5 bullets):**
- July 1, 2026: PUE ≤1.2 AND ERF (Energy Reuse Factor) ≥10% both trigger for all new German data centres — first hard dual efficiency threshold in any EU member state
- ERF escalator: ≥15% from July 1, 2027; ≥20% from July 1, 2028 — requiring progressively more waste heat routed to off-site users (district heating, industrial processes)
- January 1, 2027: 100% renewable electricity mandatory for all German data centres (all facilities, not just new-build)
- Liquid cooling paradox: AI-density workloads require liquid cooling operating at 35–45°C water temperatures — below the 70–90°C threshold needed for most district heating networks, making ERF compliance technically harder for the facilities that need it most
- France, Netherlands, Ireland, and Belgium are monitoring Germany as a legislative reference point before enacting equivalent national thresholds

**Research narrative (150–200 words):**
July 1, 2026 is the first hard efficiency checkpoint in European data centre regulation: Germany's Energy Efficiency Act now mandates PUE ≤1.2 and simultaneously triggers ERF ≥10% for all new data centres entering service. The PUE threshold was anticipated and the industry has largely adapted — but the ERF requirement opens a new compliance frontier that interacts badly with AI workload architecture.

Liquid cooling, which is effectively mandatory for high-density AI GPU racks, operates at 35–45°C coolant temperatures. Effective district heat injection for most municipal networks requires 70–90°C. The gap means liquid-cooled AI facilities cannot satisfy ERF by simply connecting to district heating; they need additional heat-upgrading infrastructure — heat pumps, heat exchangers — adding capex and operational complexity not required in air-cooled or lower-density deployments. The €5–15M premium per 50MW build for liquid cooling already flagged by the industry now compounds with ERF engineering costs.

The policy cascades matter: if France, Netherlands, or Ireland adopt equivalent ERF mandates, the Nordic advantage sharpens further — Nordic climate conditions and existing district heating networks make waste heat reuse substantially more viable.

Watch: the first cohort of German DC planning applications filed after July 1 as an empirical read on ERF compliance costs. January 2027 renewables mandate is the next milestone.
